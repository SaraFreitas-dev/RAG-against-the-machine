# 🏆 05 — BM25

**TF-IDF's grown-up sibling: saturation and length normalization you can tune**

`📚 Concepts` · `⏱️ ~25 min read` · `🎯 Prerequisite: 04 — TF-IDF`

</div>

---

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **TL;DR** — **BM25** (Best Matching 25) keeps TF-IDF's core idea, *rare terms that appear in a chunk are strong evidence*, and fixes its two biggest weaknesses with two parameters:
> - 🔁 **`k1`** controls **saturation**: how quickly repeating a term stops adding to the score;
> - 📏 **`b`** controls **length normalization**: how much a chunk is penalised for being longer than average.
>
> It has been the standard baseline for lexical search for decades, it's one of the two methods the subject accepts, and it fits this project's very uneven chunk sizes well. 🎯

## 🔙 1. What TF-IDF left unsolved

From doc 04, Section 10:

| Problem | TF-IDF's answer | Why it's not enough |
|---|---|---|
| 🔁 Repeating a word 100× shouldn't count 100× | Log-TF, sometimes | Fixed curve, nothing to tune |
| 📏 Long chunks contain more terms just by being long | Cosine normalization, sometimes | All-or-nothing, can over- or under-punish |
| 🎛️ Results are off | — | No knobs to turn |

BM25 answers all three in **one formula** with **two knobs**. 🎛️🎛️

---

## 🧮 2. The formula, at a glance

For a question *q* and a chunk *d*:

```text
                          tf(t,d) · (k1 + 1)
score(q,d) =  Σ  idf(t) · ─────────────────────────────────────
             t∈q          tf(t,d) + k1 · (1 − b + b · |d| / avgdl)
```

It looks scary 😅, but it's three ideas multiplied together, and you already know two of them:

| Piece | Symbol(s) | Idea | Section |
|---|---|---|:-:|
| 💎 **Rarity** | `idf(t)` | Rare terms are strong clues (like TF-IDF) | 3 |
| 🔁 **Saturation** | `tf`, `k1` | Repetitions help, with diminishing returns | 4 |
| 📏 **Length** | `\|d\|`, `avgdl`, `b` | Longer-than-average chunks get their TF discounted | 5 |

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `tf(t,d)` | Occurrences of term *t* in chunk *d* (doc 04) |
| `\|d\|` | Length of chunk *d*, **in terms** (after tokenization), not characters |
| `avgdl` | Average chunk length over the whole index, in terms |
| `k1` | Saturation parameter, usually between **1.2 and 2.0** |
| `b` | Length-normalization parameter, between **0 and 1**, usually **0.75** |

> [!NOTE]
> 🧮 Like TF-IDF, only terms present in **both** the question and the chunk contribute. A chunk sharing no term with the question scores 0.

## 💎 3. Piece 1: IDF, BM25-style

BM25 usually uses a slightly different IDF, coming from probability theory (the "Robertson–Spärck Jones" weight):

```text
              ⎛  N − df(t) + 0.5      ⎞
idf(t) =  ln  ⎜ ───────────────── + 1 ⎟
              ⎝    df(t) + 0.5        ⎠
```

| Part | Why it's there |
|---|---|
| `N − df` vs `df` | Compares chunks **without** the term to chunks **with** it |
| `+ 0.5` | Smoothing: avoids division by zero and extreme values |
| `+ 1` inside the log | Keeps the IDF **positive** even for terms in almost every chunk |

### 🤔 Why does the `+ 1` matter?

The original formula, without `+ 1`, gives **negative** weights to terms that appear in more than half the chunks. With N = 4 and a term in all 4 chunks:

| Version | Value | Effect |
|---|:-:|---|
| Without `+ 1`: `ln(0.5 / 4.5)` | **−2.197** | 😱 Matching the term **lowers** the score |
| With `+ 1`: `ln(0.5 / 4.5 + 1)` | **0.105** | 🤏 Tiny positive weight, as intended |

> [!TIP]
> 🐍 In code, "terms in more than half the chunks" isn't hypothetical: `self`, `def`, `return`, `import`… Know which IDF variant you use, and check how it behaves on those terms. 🔍

## 🔁 4. Piece 2: saturation and `k1`

Ignore length for a moment (pretend every chunk has average length, or `b = 0`). The TF part becomes:

```text
     tf · (k1 + 1)
    ───────────────
       tf + k1
```

### 📈 What this curve does

| tf → | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 100 | **Limit** |
|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| `k1 = 0` | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | **1** |
| `k1 = 0.5` | 1.00 | 1.20 | 1.29 | 1.36 | 1.43 | 1.49 | **1.5** |
| `k1 = 1.2` | 1.00 | 1.38 | 1.57 | 1.77 | 1.96 | 2.17 | **2.2** |
| `k1 = 2.0` | 1.00 | 1.50 | 1.80 | 2.14 | 2.50 | 2.94 | **3** |
| `k1 = 10` | 1.00 | 1.83 | 2.54 | 3.67 | 5.50 | 10.0 | **11** |

### 🔍 Reading the table

| Observation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 🎯 `tf = 1` always gives **1.00** | The first occurrence is worth the same whatever `k1` is |
| 🧱 There's a **ceiling** at `k1 + 1` | No amount of repetition can push past it: this is **saturation** |
| `k1 = 0` | Only presence matters: "does the chunk contain the term, yes or no" |
| Small `k1` | Saturates fast: the 2nd and 3rd occurrences add little |
| Large `k1` | Saturates slowly: behaves almost like raw TF |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> 🎛️ **`k1` answers one question:** *how much should repeating a term matter?* With the usual values (1.2–2.0), going from 1 to 2 occurrences helps a lot, and going from 10 to 100 barely helps.

## 📏 5. Piece 3: length normalization and `b`

### 🤔 The problem

A 1900-character chunk contains many more terms than a 200-character one. So it's more likely to contain the question's terms, and to contain them several times, **just by being long**, not because it's more relevant.

### 🔧 The fix

BM25 replaces `k1` in the denominator with an adjusted value, here called **K**:

```text
K = k1 · (1 − b + b · |d| / avgdl)
```

The ratio `|d| / avgdl` compares this chunk's length to the average:

| Chunk length | `\|d\| / avgdl` | Effect on K (when b > 0) | Effect on score |
|---|:-:|---|---|
| Shorter than average | < 1 | K **smaller** | TF counts **more** 📈 |
| Exactly average | = 1 | K = k1 | Neutral |
| Longer than average | > 1 | K **bigger** | TF counts **less** 📉 |

### 🎚️ What `b` controls

`b` decides **how much** length matters, from 0 to 1:

| `b` | Formula becomes | Meaning |
|:-:|---|---|
| **0** | K = k1 | 🙈 Length ignored completely |
| **0.75** | mostly the length ratio | ⚖️ The usual default: strong but partial normalization |
| **1** | K = k1 · \|d\| / avgdl | 📏 Full normalization: TF is effectively measured relative to length |

### 🧮 Example: same tf, different lengths

Term appearing **twice** (`tf = 2`), `k1 = 1.2`. TF-part value depending on chunk length:

| `b` ↓ / length → | ½ × avg | 1 × avg | 2 × avg | 4 × avg |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **0** | 1.38 | 1.38 | 1.38 | 1.38 |
| **0.5** | 1.52 | 1.38 | 1.16 | 0.88 |
| **0.75** | 1.60 | 1.38 | 1.07 | 0.75 |
| **1.0** | 1.69 | 1.38 | 1.00 | 0.65 |

👀 With `b = 0.75`, a chunk **4× longer** than average gets about **half** the credit for the same two occurrences, compared with an average chunk.

> [!NOTE]
> 🧭 Length normalization interacts **directly** with your chunking (doc 02). If your strategy produces a mix of tiny sections and near-2000-character functions, `b` decides how much that size difference affects ranking. 📐

## ✏️ 6. Worked example, by hand

Same tiny index as in doc 04, with `k1 = 1.2` and `b = 0.75`:

| Chunk | Terms | \|d\| |
|:-:|---|:-:|
| C1 | port · server · port · default | 4 |
| C2 | server · start · model | 3 |
| C3 | model · config · port | 3 |
| C4 | model · load · model · weights | 4 |

`N = 4` · `avgdl = (4 + 3 + 3 + 4) / 4 = 3.5`

### 1️⃣ IDF (BM25-style)

| Term | df | Computation | **idf** |
|---|:-:|---|:-:|
| default | 1 | ln((4 − 1 + 0.5) / (1 + 0.5) + 1) = ln(3.333) | **1.204** |
| port | 2 | ln((4 − 2 + 0.5) / (2 + 0.5) + 1) = ln(2) | **0.693** |

### 2️⃣ Length factor K for each matching chunk

| Chunk | \|d\| / avgdl | K = 1.2 · (0.25 + 0.75 · ratio) |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| C1 | 4 / 3.5 = 1.143 | **1.329** |
| C3 | 3 / 3.5 = 0.857 | **1.071** |

### 3️⃣ Score the question *"default port"*

| Chunk | Term | tf | idf × tf·(k1+1) / (tf + K) | Contribution |
|:-:|---|:-:|---|:-:|
| C1 | default | 1 | 1.204 × 2.2 / 2.329 | 1.137 |
| C1 | port | 2 | 0.693 × 4.4 / 3.329 | 0.916 |
| | | | **C1 total** | **2.054** 🥇 |
| C3 | port | 1 | 0.693 × 2.2 / 2.071 | **0.736** 🥈 |
| C2, C4 | — | 0 | — | **0** |

**Ranking:** C1 → C3 → (C2, C4). Same order as TF-IDF here: on a clean example, both agree. ✅ The difference shows up on **messy** data. 👇

## 😈 7. The repetition chunk, revisited

Add doc 04's silly chunk again:

```text
C5: port port port port port port port port port port     (10 × "port", |d| = 10)
```

Now `N = 5`, `avgdl = 4.8`, `idf(port) = 0.539`, `idf(default) = 1.386`.

| Chunk | TF-IDF, raw TF (doc 04) | TF-IDF, log TF (doc 04) | **BM25** (`k1 = 1.2`, `b = 0.75`) |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| C1 (the real answer) | 2.631 | 2.474 🥇 | **2.265** 🥇 |
| C5 (spam) | 5.108 🥇 😱 | 1.687 | **0.974** |

Why BM25 crushes C5:

| Effect | On C5 |
|---|---|
| 🔁 **Saturation** | 10 occurrences ≈ only ~2× the value of one, never more than `k1 + 1 = 2.2` |
| 📏 **Length** | C5 is twice the average length, so K grows to **2.175** and the TF part shrinks further |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> 🎯 This is the core of BM25's advantage: **both** mechanisms push the same way against chunks that score high only by being long or repetitive. In a real corpus that means large boilerplate code files, long tables, or lists. 📋

## 🎛️ 8. Tuning `k1` and `b`

### 📍 Starting point

The classic defaults, **`k1 ≈ 1.2–2.0`** and **`b = 0.75`**, come from decades of experiments on **English prose** (news, web pages). Your corpus is **half code**, with very uneven chunk sizes. The defaults are a reasonable start, not a guarantee. 🤷

### 🔬 How to tune

1. 🎛️ Pick a small grid, e.g. `k1 ∈ {0.9, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0}` × `b ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9}`.
2. 🔁 For each pair, run `search_dataset` and evaluate recall@5.
3. 📊 Look at **docs and code separately**: they may prefer different values.
4. 📝 Record the results for your README's "Performance analysis".

> [!TIP]
> ⚡ Changing `k1` or `b` doesn't require re-chunking or re-tokenizing. Whether it requires re-**indexing** depends on what your index stores (doc 06). If tuning is slow, that's a hint about your index design. 🧩

### ⚠️ Overfitting

> [!WARNING]
> 🎯 If you tune hard on the **public** datasets, you may pick values that work great on those questions and worse on others. The defense uses reference datasets, and you can't assume they're identical. Prefer settings that are **good across both** datasets over settings that are perfect on one. Big gains from tuning usually mean something else (chunking, tokenization) deserves attention. 🔍

## 🔗 9. How BM25 interacts with your other choices

BM25 doesn't work in isolation. Choices from earlier docs change its inputs:

| Earlier choice | Affects | How |
|---|---|---|
| ✂️ **Chunk size** (doc 02) | `\|d\|`, `avgdl` | Changes what "long" means, and how much `b` matters |
| 🔁 **Overlap** (doc 02) | `tf`, `df` | Shared text is counted in several chunks: `df` grows for those terms |
| 🔤 **Emitting identifier parts** (doc 03) | `tf`, `\|d\|` | `max_num_seqs` → 4 terms: code chunks get longer in terms, and `max` gets extra occurrences |
| 🚮 **Stopwords** (doc 03) | `\|d\|`, `avgdl` | Removing them shortens every chunk |
| 🧭 **Extra context in indexed text** (doc 02, Section 8) | `tf`, `\|d\|` | Adding a heading or file name to each chunk adds terms and length |

> [!NOTE]
> 🔬 That's why "change one thing at a time" (doc 02, Section 10) matters: a tokenizer change can shift the best `b` value, because it changes chunk lengths.

### ❓ Repeated words in the question

If a question repeats a term (*"port … which port …"*), should it count twice? The basic formula sums over **query terms**, so it depends on whether you treat the question as a list (repetitions count) or a set (each term once). Some BM25 variants add a third parameter for this. Questions are short, so it rarely matters, but **decide consciously**. 🤔

## 🧩 10. Variants and libraries

"BM25" is also a small family:

| Variant | Difference |
|---|---|
| **Okapi BM25** | The classic formula above (with one of several IDF versions) |
| **BM25+** | Adds a small constant so long chunks containing a term never score *lower* than chunks without it |
| **BM25L** | Adjusts length normalization to penalise long documents less |
| **BM25F** | Weights several fields differently (e.g. title vs body), relevant if you index a heading separately from the text |

> [!WARNING]
> 📚 Libraries exist (for example `rank-bm25` on PyPI), and they differ in **IDF variant**, handling of negative IDF, and defaults. If you use one, read its source or docs so you can explain **exactly** what it computes, and remember that the subject asks you to **implement** TF-IDF or BM25: check with your peers what's accepted. 🤝 Implementing BM25 yourself is also a great way to be ready for the defense: the formula fits on one line once the counts are prepared.

## ⚖️ 11. TF-IDF vs BM25

| | 📊 TF-IDF | 🏆 BM25 |
|---|---|---|
| **Rarity** | IDF | IDF (probabilistic variant) |
| **Repetition** | Linear, or log if you choose | Built-in **saturation**, tunable with `k1` |
| **Length** | None, or cosine normalization | Built-in, tunable with `b` |
| **Parameters** | None | `k1`, `b` |
| **Explainability** | ✅ Very simple | ✅ Simple once each piece is understood |
| **Speed** | ⚡ Fast | ⚡ Fast (same counts, a few more operations) |
| **Typical quality** | Good baseline | Usually better, especially with uneven lengths |
| **Shared limits** | Bag of words · exact terms only · no synonyms (doc 03, Section 9) | Same |

## 🚫 12. Common misconceptions

| ❌ Myth | ✅ Reality |
|---|---|
| "BM25 understands meaning better than TF-IDF." | It's still **lexical**: it only sees exact term matches. It just weighs them more sensibly. |
| "`\|d\|` is the chunk length in characters." | It's measured in **terms**, after tokenization. |
| "`k1` makes the score bigger or smaller." | It controls how **fast** repetitions saturate. The first occurrence always counts 1 × idf. |
| "`b = 1` is best: full fairness to short chunks." | Full normalization can over-favour tiny chunks with one lucky match. That's why the default is 0.75. |
| "The default parameters are optimal." | They're good defaults for English prose. Your corpus is half code: measure. |
| "IDF in BM25 can't be negative." | It can, without the `+ 1` term, for terms in more than half the chunks. |
| "Tuning `k1` and `b` is the main lever for recall." | Usually small gains. Chunking and tokenization tend to matter much more. |
